research

I work on the literature and culture of the long nineteenth century, particularly the Victorian novel and its relationship to cultural and social life. More specifically, I'm interested in the work of narrative form, especially the relationship between serial form and the history of the novel, and the relationship between novel form and emerging theories of human identity. I am the co-director (with Adam Grener) of Dickens Notes, which explores Charles Dickens's working notes to demonstrate the serial unfolding of his novels and the dynamic networks of interaction between characters and readers--and between notes and novel--they facilitate. You can read more about my current projects below.

Dickens Notes

I am the co-director of Dickens Notes, an open-access digital scholarly edition of the detailed working notes Charles Dickens kept for his novels in the second half of his career. These texts constitute a unique archive essential to understanding this major author's navigation of serial writing in monthly or weekly installments. In addition to offering valuable insights into individual novels, these documents shed light on the development of serial narrative form in the 19th century. Our interactive edition presents visually-faithful transcriptions of Dickens's manuscripts and comprehensive annotations to capture Dickens's dynamic use of these pages before, during, and after writing his installments.

Dickens Notes is supported by a 2026-27 NEH Scholarly Editions grant to fund the completion of the project, including the publication of the remaining five editions of notes and the integration of manuscript photographs.

I co-direct this project alongside Adam Grener, Senior Lecturer in the English Programme at Te Herenga Waka—Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand. The project was peer reviewed by Beverley Park Rilett in Reviews in Digital Humanities in September 2024.

The question "What is Victorian Literature?" opened the description for a graduate course I taught in spring 2025, an invitation to limn the scope of our field. From the beginning of the semester, my students wisely resisted the definitional impulse that it presupposed—the notion that our goal was to identify a coherent object of study, albeit one that would expand beyond our expected list of "major" authors to include authors of color and writers living within and moving through the expansive colonial geography of Britain. As our semester began to unfold amid the dismantling of diversity programs and anti-racist initiatives that had been lauded so enthusiastically only a few semesters earlier, our class took up this question as an invitation to define our orientation towards our objects of study, our field, and the work of the humanities more broadly. In this essay, I reflect on the lessons I learned from teaching this class in this moment, from my own inadequate and provisional, but productive, attempt to design a course that answered the call by Alicia Mireless Christoff, Amy R. Wong, and Ronjaunee Chatterjee to "undiscipline" our field. In the wake of that call, and in the classroom where that call is tested, the task is not (only) to expand boundaries but to interrogate, with our students, the methodological and representational components of Victorian studies as a classroom practice. Annotation is such an everyday readerly activity that it is easy to dismiss as too mundane and minute to have serious value as scholarship. And yet social annotation has seen a resurgence as a pedagogical strategy thanks to new digital tools, and Victorian Studies scholars were encouraged to engage in annotative engagement with one another's papers in COVE Studio during EVENT 2024. While the uneven uptake and mixed success of annotation over the course of the conference year might suggest that the margins are not yet the vibrant virtual spaces of possibility they seem to be in our teaching, this essay asserts the value of the margins as pleasurable spaces of vital critical practice. I explore the affordances of annotation for facilitating fluid, even emancipatory methods of critical engagement that can bridge the pedagogical and scholarly realms of our academic work. Tracing recent attempts to theorise annotation in the digital era alongside trends in open scholarship, and addressing the structural impediments to collaborative and flexible forms of writing, I argue that our field should embrace annotation as a valuable form of scholarly practice and scholarly product. The Digital Dickens Notes Project (DDNP) is a digital initiative that seeks to transcribe, interpret, and explore the significance of the working notes Charles Dickens kept for most of his novels. Our online platform (dickensnotes.com) presents color transcriptions that display Dickens's use of space, ink colors, and nontextual markings to capture his intricate use of these pages over the course of the many weeks or months he planned, composed, and published his novels serially. Pairing these transcriptions with comprehensive editorial annotations and critical introductions, the DDNP aims to shed new light on the temporal dynamics of these working notes as fascinating records of serial composition, and in the process to open new avenues and methodologies for analyzing Dickens's works. This co-authored article introduces the DDNP and its methodology. Link
This essay reads Charlotte Brontë's use of first-person narration in Villette as a contribution to a Victorian reassessment of personal identity as material, heterogeneous, and adaptive. Challenging common readings of Brontë's first-person fictions as displays of self-definition and authority, I unpack the relationship between the narrated and narrating person in both Jane Eyre and Villette to reveal dual operations of narrative—world-making strategies and adaptive tactics—that express competing notions of personhood. By comparing Villette (1853) to Jane Eyre (1847), we can chart a shift from a strategic to a tactical emphasis in narration indicative, I argue, of a broader movement in nineteenth-century thought from Cartesian dualism toward associationist and materialist theories of consciousness as adaptive and processual. Villette's narrative tactics demonstrate the potential in novel form to enact processes of being in the world that challenge both traditional concepts of a unified, self-contained consciousness and new Victorian scientific conceptions of a material mind only knowable from science's "objective" perspective. Positioning Brontë's novel form alongside psychological debates about the nature and study of mind, I show how the novel offers alternative methodologies and conclusions about the nature of personhood to those proffered by an emerging Victorian psychology. When Brontë's first-person narrative produces (rather than assumes the prior presence of) a "person" narrating, it asks us to read that production of identity and consciousness as an experiment with what it means to experience oneself as, in the words of Jane Eyre, a "heterogeneous thing." This essay demonstrates how Charles Dickens used the form of serial fiction to experiment with a uniquely Victorian idea of life as a dynamic network of interactions. Reading Our Mutual Friend alongside nineteenth-century physiological and evolutionary writing, I show how Dickens shaped novel form around the attractions and reactions that organized social and psychological life in his city. Victorian sciences—particularly the work of George Henry Lewes and Charles Darwin—were turning to "net-work" to describe the plastic processes underpinning biological life. Dickens's fiction used the same dynamic potential of network form to put in motion the mechanisms of social life. Taking a novel form traditionally organized around the story of a self, Dickens used serial publication to incorporate variety, change, and new combinations of radically heterogeneous characters into each new installment, turning novel form into an ongoing formation. The resulting model of character psychology is one that replaces interiority with interaction and individuated desire with physiological affect. Link

Forthcoming Articles and Chapters

This contribution to the Routledge Companion to Sensation Fiction (projected publication 2026) offers a pedagogical approach to using "sensation" as both an object of study and as an opportunity to interrogate our orientation, as 21st-century readers, towards Victorian cultural objects. Drawing on courses taught at two institutions, I present a series of three scaffolded "explorations" that contextualize sensation fiction and engage students in discussions about the work of interpretation. These lessons culminate in a digital curation project for which each student selects, presents, and analyzes their own Victorian "sensational object." This method helps students engage with broad cultural ideas of the sensational, while using sensation as a means of reflecting on our practices of reading, analysis, and critique.
This contribution to the edited collection Digital Dickens (White Rose Press, projected publication 2026) explores the work of digital annotation that underpins the Dickens Notes project. Co-authored with Adam Grener, this essay considers how an interactive, exploratory edition of these working notes can foster a user-experience that imitates how the author himself used these pages. We explain the affordances of a digital platform for cultivating scholars' understanding and readers' appreciation of the dynamic temporality of Dickens's serial composition. Focusing on the working notes for Hard Times and Little Dorrit, the chapter discusses the opportunities and challenges of using digital annotation to make the complexity of these static documents visible. While for Dickens the working notes served as a compressed record of an ongoing compositional process, the Dickens Notes annotations use these records to access that process and to offer new points of entry into and exploration of Dickens's serial novels.
This contribution to the MLA Approaches to Teaching Great Expectations volume considers how to engage students with the serial form of Dickens's weekly novel. It offers practical methods for encountering the material facets of the novel's seriality from the outset of teaching the novel. By training students' attention on the breaks between parts and their affective and formal dimensions as they read, and encouraging them to reflect on the relationship between parts and whole at the end, I offer ways of attending to the serial dynamics of a novel they otherwise might imagine as a large single text. The essay makes the case that attention to the serial format and form of the novel helps students resist the priority of the holistic critical vantage point and both interrogate and value their own responses to the novel as they read.

Book Project

The Novel in Process: Charles Dickens and Serial Form

co-authored with Adam Grener

Charles Dickens was still in the process of conceiving and writing his novels as his first readers were already experiencing them, installment by serial installment. And yet scholars and readers today tend to regard Dickens as an author of completed books. By returning us to the active scenes of Dickens's composition, The Novel in Process challenges the whole text as the privileged object of literary analysis. This book demonstrates instead how the novel's form depends on the dynamics of its writing processes—captured in manuscripts, notes, memoranda, letters, proofs—which reveal the importance of openness and contingency to how novels are composed and read. As we show with new research into Dickens's manuscript archives, these novels were uniquely shaped by the constraints and rhythms of serial publication. This book reimagines Dickens's novels through the forces that gave them shape—from the integration of characters and storylines through the distended process of serial publication, to the spatial parameters of the manuscript page, the typeset column, the printed installment. The Novel in Process offers new perspectives on Dickens's major novels and career, and, in so doing, articulates a process-based theory of the novel itself.
